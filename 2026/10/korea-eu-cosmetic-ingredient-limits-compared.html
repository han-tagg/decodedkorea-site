Korea and the EU Both Restrict 535 Cosmetic Ingredients. Where Their Limits Split

October 28, 2026 · K-Beauty: Ingredients, K-Beauty: Regulations

In the MFDS export-market data, 535 cosmetic ingredients carry a concentration limit or condition in both Korea and the EU. For a formulator taking a Korean product to Europe, most of that overlap is uneventful: where the two maximum concentrations can be read on the same basis, they are usually identical. The differences that matter are fewer and easy to miss.

Some are EU limits lower than Korea's, set for a product type or a sub-group that the Korean entry does not separate. Others are ingredients Korea still allows up to a limit and the EU has moved to its list of prohibited substances. This post sets out both, with the Korean rule text from MFDS Notice No. 2026-19 and the EU entries from CosIng and EUR-Lex.

Where both markets set a limit

For 258 of the 535 ingredients, each Korean maximum could be paired with an EU maximum for the same product type. In 247 of them the numbers are the same. Many of these come from shared group entries: 118 ingredients fall under the entry for trialkylamines, trialkanolamines and their salts (2.5% in leave-on products), 34 under water-soluble zinc salts (1% as zinc), and 10 under the parabens entry (0.4% for a single ester, 0.8% for mixtures, as acid).

In the other 11, Korea's maximum is lower than the EU's. They belong to six entries:

p-Chloro-m-Cresol 0.04%, not on mucous membranes 0.2%, not on mucous membranes
Propionic acid and five of its salts 0.9% as propionic acid 2% (acid)
Phenylbenzimidazole Sulfonic Acid 4% 8% (as acid)

None of the 258 cleanly paired ingredients has a lower EU maximum. The other 277 could not be paired that way, mostly because the two texts split product types differently (152 ingredients) or give no maximum concentration at all (85, largely colourants), and the EU limits that are lower than Korea's are in that group.

Where the EU limit is lower

Propylparaben and butylparaben are the main case. Korea covers them in its general parabens entry: 0.4% for a single ester and 0.8% for mixtures, as acid. The EU gives them their own entry: 0.14% as acid for the sum of propylparaben, butylparaben and their salts, within the 0.8% total for parabens, and no use in leave-on products for the nappy area of children under three. A leave-on product with 0.3% propylparaben meets the Korean limit and exceeds the EU one. The same applies to the sodium and potassium salts of both esters, six ingredients in all.

Octocrylene is the other. Korea allows 10% in any product. The EU allows 10% in most products but 0.9% in propellant sprays, so a Korean spray sunscreen formulated near the Korean limit needs reformulating for Europe.

Oxidative hair dyes need separate handling. For eight of them the EU figure is lower on the raw numbers, among them m-Aminophenol (2% in Korea, 1.2% in the EU), 4-Nitro-o-Phenylenediamine (1.5% and 0.5%) and p-Phenylenediamine sulfate (3.8% and 2% as free base). The EU limits apply to the concentration on the hair after mixing under oxidative conditions, and the Korean text does not state that basis, so these numbers should not be compared directly without checking how each was measured.

Allowed in Korea, prohibited in the EU

The data has 41 ingredients with a Korean limit and an EU prohibition entry. For 29 of them the EU prohibition is conditional: 25 are banned only when used as a substance in hair dye products, and the rest depend on formaldehyde release, nano form, fragrance use or furocoumarin content. That leaves 12 ingredients the EU bans without conditions. One is rh-Oligopeptide-1, covered in our post on EGF, and one, Zinc Borate, is linked on the Korean side through a group entry we have not been able to confirm applies to it. The other ten form seven entries, and all seven sit in Annex 2 of the Korean regulation, the list of ingredients allowed with restrictions. None of them appears in Annex 1, the Korean list of prohibited ingredients.

The EU dates matter for anyone still selling older stock. Isobutylparaben and Isopropylparaben could not be placed on the EU market from 30 October 2014 or made available from 30 July 2015. The Zinc Pyrithione prohibition has applied since 1 March 2022. For 4-Methylbenzylidene Camphor, products could not be placed on the market from 1 May 2025, and from 1 May 2026 they can no longer be made available, so stock already in European shops is now covered too. Entry 1397 for perborates was replaced by Regulation (EU) 2026/78, which applies from 1 May 2026.

The last column of the table shows that the EU is not always alone. For Zinc Pyrithione and 4-Methylbenzylidene Camphor it is the only market of the ten with a prohibition in the data; China, ASEAN, Taiwan, Brazil and Argentina restrict both. For the two parabens, Quaternium-15 and Chlorophene, a formula built to the Korean limit also fails in China, ASEAN, Brazil and Argentina. The parabens case is easy to miss in Korea because both esters sit inside the general parabens entry, next to methylparaben and propylparaben, rather than on a line of their own.

Prohibited in Korea, restricted in the EU, often the same rule

The reverse combination looks larger: 167 ingredients have a Korean prohibition and an EU restriction. Most of them are not a difference in substance. Korea often writes a condition as a prohibition of the material that fails it, while the EU writes the same condition as a restriction on the material that passes it.

Ninety-seven of the 167 are linked to Korean entries that prohibit amines or alkanolamines with a secondary amine content above 0.5%. The matching EU restriction sets a maximum secondary amine content of 0.5%. Another 44 are linked to Korean entries that prohibit materials with a peroxide value above a threshold. Limonene is one: Korea prohibits d-, dl- and l-limonene with a peroxide value above 20 mmol/L, and the EU requires a peroxide value below 20 mmol/L. Oils and extracts of Pinus sylvestris and Thuja occidentalis follow the same pattern at 10 mmol/L. Methyl eugenol goes one step further. Korea lists it as prohibited unless it occurs naturally from plant extracts below a set of limits by product type, and those limits (0.01% in fine fragrance down to 0.0002% in other leave-on products) are the same figures the EU uses as its restriction.

Counting these as cases where Korea is stricter would overstate the gap. For a formulator, the question in both markets is whether the material meets the purity or peroxide specification.

Checking a Korean formula for the EU

Search the formula for the seven entries in the second table first. A product built to the Korean limits can contain any of them legally in Korea, and none can go to the EU. For preservatives, check the paraben esters one by one rather than relying on the general parabens limit. Then check propylparaben and butylparaben against the EU's 0.14% and the nappy-area rule, and spray products against the EU's lower octocrylene limit. For other ingredients both markets restrict, the Korean and EU maximums are usually the same, but confirm the product type split before relying on that, and treat oxidative hair dyes separately because the two texts measure them differently. For ingredients Korea prohibits on purity grounds, the EU condition is usually the same specification, so the supplier's certificate of analysis answers both.
