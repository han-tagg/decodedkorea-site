Banned in Europe, Legal in Korea: 5 Ingredients That Split Global Regulators

Published
April 14, 2026
Topics
  • K-Beauty: Regulations
  • K-Beauty: Ingredients

The same moisturizer that sits on a shelf in Seoul could be pulled from a store in Paris. Not because of a labeling error or a packaging defect — but because an ingredient inside is classified differently by two governments.

This happens more often than most consumers realize. The EU and South Korea — both major cosmetics markets with sophisticated regulatory systems — frequently disagree on where to draw the line. The EU's cosmetics regulation (EC 1223/2009) maintains one of the strictest banned substance lists in the world, with over 1,600 entries in Annex II. Korea's MFDS takes a different approach, often allowing the same ingredients under specific concentration limits or without restriction.

Neither system is wrong. Where the two part ways, the EU is usually the one that bans outright and Korea the one that sets a limit. In our database, 14 ingredients are prohibited in the EU without conditions while Korea sets a limit for them, and we found no ingredient that Korea prohibits outright while the EU only limits it.

Here are five ingredients that illustrate this divide.

1. Cyclotetrasiloxane (D4)

What it is: A cyclic silicone commonly used in primers, foundations, and hair serums. It gives products that smooth, silky slip K-Beauty is famous for.

CAS No.: 556-67-2

EU status: Banned. In June 2018, the Member State Committee of the European Chemicals Agency identified D4 as a substance of very high concern (SVHC) because it is persistent, bioaccumulative and toxic (PBT) and very persistent and very bioaccumulative (vPvB). As of 2020, D4 is restricted in wash-off products above 0.1%, and the ban expanded further in subsequent amendments. ASEAN also bans D4.

Korea status: Restricted at 8.7%. D4 remains permitted in Korean cosmetic formulations, but since March 3, 2026 it has been capped at 8.7% under Annex 2 of the MFDS Regulation on Safety Standards for Cosmetics (Notice No. 2025-63). It appears across a wide range of product categories — from base makeup to skincare serums.

Who bans it, who doesn't:

Market Status
EU Banned
ASEAN Banned
Korea Restricted (8.7%)

Only two regulatory blocs have moved to ban D4. The rest of the world — including Korea — has not.

Why the split: The EU acts on D4 under two laws. Under the Cosmetics Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2019/831 added D4 to Annex II because it is classified as toxic for reproduction (category 2) and no request for an exception was submitted. Under REACH, its restrictions in cosmetic products rest on its PBT and vPvB properties (Regulations (EU) 2018/35 and 2024/1328). Korea caps D4 at 8.7% instead of banning it.

2. 4-Methylbenzylidene Camphor (4-MBC)

What it is: A chemical UV filter used in sunscreens and daily moisturizers with SPF protection.

CAS No.: 36861-47-9

EU status: Banned. The EU removed 4-MBC from the list of permitted UV filters following a reassessment by the Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS), citing concerns about endocrine disruption.

Korea status: Restricted at 4%. Korea's MFDS classifies 4-MBC as a functional cosmetic ingredient (기능성화장품 성분) for UV protection and permits its use up to 4% concentration.

Who bans it, who doesn't:

Market Status
EU Banned
Korea Restricted — max 4%
Taiwan Restricted — max 4%
China Restricted — max 4%
ASEAN Restricted — max 4%
Brazil Restricted — max 4%
Argentina Restricted — max 4%
Canada Restricted — max 4%

This one stands out. The EU is the only major market that has banned 4-MBC entirely. Every other regulatory body examined — Korea, Taiwan, China, ASEAN, Brazil, Argentina, Canada — allows it at 4% concentration.

Why the split: UV filter regulation is one of the most fragmented areas in global cosmetics. Korea and six other markets allow 4-MBC at up to 4%. In the EU, the SCCS could not conclude that 4-MBC is safe and found that no maximum concentration for safe use could be derived, noting sufficient evidence that it may act as an endocrine disruptor. Regulation (EU) 2024/996 then prohibited it in all cosmetic products. When seven out of eight regulators permit an ingredient and one bans it, the question is less about "who's right" and more about where each system draws the line.

3. Butylphenyl Methylpropional (Lilial)

What it is: A synthetic fragrance ingredient with a fresh, floral scent. Widely used in perfumed creams, body lotions, and shampoos across the global cosmetics industry for decades.

CAS No.: 80-54-6

EU status: Banned (March 2022). Lilial was added to Annex II (prohibited substances) of the EU Cosmetics Regulation after it was classified as a Category 1B reproductive toxicant under CLP Regulation. This classification was based on animal studies showing adverse effects on fertility. ASEAN has also banned Lilial.

Korea status: Restricted at 0.14%. Since March 3, 2026, Korea's MFDS has capped Lilial at 0.14% under Annex 2 of the Regulation on Safety Standards for Cosmetics (Notice No. 2025-63). It continues to appear in fragranced K-Beauty products across multiple categories.

Who bans it, who doesn't:

Market Status
EU Banned
ASEAN Banned
Korea Restricted (0.14%)

Why the split: Under Article 15(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, a substance classified as CMR (carcinogenic, mutagenic, or reprotoxic) Category 1A or 1B is prohibited in cosmetics unless an exception is granted. No exception was requested for Lilial, and Regulation (EU) 2021/1902 added it to Annex II. Korea limits Lilial to 0.14% rather than prohibiting it.

The industry impact was significant: Lilial's EU ban forced reformulations across thousands of products in markets that follow EU classifications. In Korea, Lilial is limited to 0.14% rather than banned, while ASEAN prohibits it as the EU does.

4. Colloidal Gold

What it is: Suspended gold particles marketed in premium K-Beauty products for anti-aging and brightening. Gold sheet masks and gold-infused serums are a signature category of Korean luxury skincare.

CAS No.: 7440-57-5

EU status: It depends on the form. The EU does not simply "ban" gold. Gold is a permitted colorant, while gold in nano form is prohibited: - As a coloring agent: Restricted — permitted under purity criteria set out in Commission Directive 95/45/EC (E175). - In nano form, including Colloidal Gold (nano): Banned under Annex II for any use (entry 1728, added by Regulation (EU) 2024/858).

In other words, gold can be used to color a cosmetic product in the EU, but nano gold cannot be used in one at all.

Korea status: Restricted (permitted). Korea allows colloidal gold in cosmetic products as a restricted ingredient with no use-type limitation. Korean formulators use it freely in both decorative and skincare applications.

Who restricts it, and how:

Market Status Notes
EU Banned / Restricted Banned in nano form; allowed as coloring agent with purity criteria
Korea Restricted Permitted across product types
Taiwan Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
China Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
ASEAN Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
Brazil Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
Argentina Restricted Permitted as coloring agent

Why the split: The EU allows gold as a colorant (CI 77480) under the purity criteria for the food additive E175, and prohibits gold in nano form. Regulation (EU) 2024/858 added nano gold to Annex II after the SCCS found the available information too limited for a safety assessment and concluded that its use in cosmetics can pose a health risk. Korea's entry for gold in our data carries no use-type condition.

5. Colloidal Silver

What it is: Suspended silver particles used in cosmetics for their antimicrobial properties. Popular in Korean products targeting acne-prone and sensitive skin — found in acne patches, antibacterial mists, and sensitive-skin treatments.

CAS No.: 7440-22-4

EU status: Same dual structure as gold. - As a coloring agent: Restricted — permitted under purity criteria set out in Commission Directive 95/45/EC (E174). - As colloidal silver in nano form: Banned under Annex II for any use (entry 1727, added by Regulation (EU) 2024/858).

Korea status: Restricted (permitted). Korea allows colloidal silver in cosmetic products as a restricted ingredient.

Who restricts it, and how:

Market Status Notes
EU Banned / Restricted Banned in nano form; allowed as coloring agent with purity criteria
Korea Restricted Permitted
Taiwan Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
US Restricted Fingernail polish only, max 1%; no eye area or general use
China Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
ASEAN Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
Brazil Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
Argentina Restricted Permitted as coloring agent
Canada Restricted Mouthwash max 0.04%; requires labeling about silver content

Colloidal silver shows the widest regulatory fragmentation of all five ingredients. Each market draws the line in a different place — the US restricts it to nail polish, Canada allows it in mouthwash with warnings, the EU splits by intended use, and Korea permits it broadly.

Why the split: Silver's antimicrobial properties are well-documented, but the markets differ on where they allow it. The EU prohibited colloidal silver in nano form through the same Regulation (EU) 2024/858, on the finding that the data were insufficient to assess its safety. Korea's entry for silver in our data carries no use-type condition. The US takes a uniquely narrow approach, limiting silver to a single product category.

What This Actually Means

Five ingredients. Multiple regulatory systems. Different conclusions.

The instinct is to ask "who's right?" — but that misses the point. These disagreements do not reflect negligence on either side. They follow from how each system's rules are written:

  • The EU's approach: A hazard classification or an unresolved safety question can be enough for a ban. D4 and Lilial went into Annex II after being classified as toxic for reproduction, with no request for an exception, and 4-MBC was banned after the SCCS could not establish a safe concentration. That made the EU the only market in this comparison to ban 4-MBC, which seven others allow at up to 4%.
  • Korea's approach: Korea sets concentration limits for D4, Lilial and 4-MBC instead of banning them, and its entries for gold and silver carry no use-type condition.
  • The "it depends" approach (EU on gold/silver): Some ingredients aren't simply banned or allowed — their regulatory status depends on the form. Gold and silver are permitted colorants, while nano gold and colloidal silver in nano form are prohibited in any cosmetic product.

For consumers shopping across borders — especially those buying K-Beauty products online — the practical takeaway is straightforward: a product being "banned in the EU" does not automatically mean it is unsafe. It means an EU rule prohibits the ingredient, which for these five rests on a hazard classification or on an SCCS opinion that could not confirm safety. Korea, and often many other markets, draw the line elsewhere.

The more useful question is not "is this ingredient banned somewhere?" but rather "what is the actual regulatory status in each market, and what evidence supports each position?"

Methodology and Sources

The regulatory data in this article is cross-referenced against a structured database of 21,796 cosmetic ingredients with regulatory records spanning 10 countries and 31,191 regulatory entries:

  • EU: Cosmetics Regulation EC 1223/2009, Annex II (Prohibited Substances) and Annex IV (Colorants)
  • Korea: MFDS Cosmetic Ingredient Standards (식품의약품안전처 화장품 원료 기준)
  • Other markets: Regulatory entries for Taiwan, the US, China, ASEAN, Brazil, Argentina and Canada come from the restriction data MFDS compiles for Korea and nine export markets
  • Ingredient records: compiled in March 2026
